The land

Before 2024 the site could not be walked. Japanese barberry, Japanese stiltgrass, non-native evergreens and Japanese maples covered the whole holding. Clearance ran April to June 2024 and planting followed immediately. The work was divided into four zones along a long, narrow parcel that runs from a reservoir shore at one end to a septic drain field at the other.

What the microphone is weighted toward

Zone B sits closest, inside twenty metres, and it is the richest and busiest part of the site: dense native shrub and herbaceous planting under full deer protection. Nothing else there draws birds, since the swimming pool is salt water and the house offers no eaves or nest sites, so the activity is a response to the planting alone. It falls inside every detection radius, including for the quietest high-pitched singers, so it carries more of this record than any other zone. It is also the one planted area where nothing was browsed, which should make it the fastest to develop structure.

Zone C, from twenty to a hundred and ten metres, is the largest and was the most degraded. It is unfenced. Browsing has occurred, but the understory is regenerating and a wide range of the plantings have taken hold. A small collecting pool there is the only drinking and bathing water on the property. The mature oak canopy was retained across the site, and oaks carry a larger caterpillar fauna than any other tree genus in North America, so the insect base that canopy and bark feeders rely on was never disturbed. What the project added was structure underneath it.

Zone D sits at the far margin of hearing range. Birds there register only if they sing loudly or from open ground, which the meadow provides.

What lives here, month by month

The site holds two largely different bird communities across the year, and the difference tells you what the restoration has and has not yet achieved.

Eighty-eight species are present in the breeding season, 75 in autumn and 50 in winter. Twenty-nine are here in all four seasons: five woodpeckers, five raptors and owls, three corvids, and the familiar residents — Black-capped Chickadee, Tufted Titmouse, White-breasted Nuthatch, Brown Creeper, Northern Cardinal, American Robin, Song Sparrow and Cedar Waxwing. Forty-five come only to breed. Just seven come only for the winter, among them Dark-eyed Junco, White-throated Sparrow, Red-breasted Nuthatch and Pine Siskin.

Sorting those birds by how they feed shows where the turnover falls. In June and July the dominant group is birds that hunt invertebrates on the ground and in the shrub layer: twenty species, more than any other group. Ovenbird and Worm-eating Warbler walk the leaf litter. Wood Thrush, Veery and Eastern Towhee turn it over. Hooded Warbler and Blue-winged Warbler work the low shrubs above them.

By January seventeen of those twenty are gone. American Robin, Carolina Wren and Hermit Thrush are the only ones that stay, and no winter specialist arrives to fill the gap; the canopy and aerial insectivores disappear altogether. What holds on is bark and seed: nine species working trunks and branches, among them six woodpeckers, both nuthatches and Brown Creeper, and eleven on seed heads and mast, led by Dark-eyed Junco, White-throated Sparrow, Black-capped Chickadee and Tufted Titmouse.

Migration timing has not shifted. Comparing first spring arrival dates for 24 long-distance migrants between 2025 and 2026, twelve came earlier and eleven came later, with a median change of zero days. Whatever is changing here is not phenology.

House Wren and Wood Thrush

House Wren is the clearest single case. In summer 2024 it was not once detected heavily enough in a day to count as present on that day; by 2025 it was on 28 of 54 days. It needs dense low cover with a cavity close by. The house offers no nest sites, so the cavities are natural ones in the retained trees, and what changed beneath them is the cover. Sound alone cannot say which zone it settled in.

Wood Thrush matters more. It is a Partners in Flight watch-list species in long-term regional decline, and it needs a shrubby understory beneath a closed canopy to nest in. It went from 0.43 to 0.98 of breeding-season days, and in June 2026 it sang on 24 of 28 days with a nineteen-day unbroken run and 43% of its detections in the dawn chorus. That is a territory, not a visitor.

The gains track what the birds eat

Grouped by diet rather than by where they forage, the 111 credible species separate cleanly, and the separation points at the food web rather than at physical cover.

Every group that gained eats insects or fruit. Every flat group eats seeds, nuts or carrion — resources the project never altered, since the mature oak canopy and its mast crop were left standing. Raptors and owls gained as well, which is the same argument one level further up: more insects support more songbirds and small mammals, which support more predators.

Two results make better sense under a food explanation than a structural one. Canopy gleaners gained 0.091 and bark foragers 0.078, yet the canopy and the trunks beneath it were never touched. If the mechanism were physical cover in the shrub layer, neither group had any reason to respond. If the mechanism is arthropod supply, both did: clearing barberry and stiltgrass releases the invertebrate community across the whole site, and several hundred native plantings add host plants for the specialist herbivores that most non-native shrubs cannot support.

The flycatchers sharpen it. Those that hunt flying insects from perches inside the vegetation all rose — Great Crested Flycatcher from 0.490 to 0.808, Acadian Flycatcher from absent to 0.173, Eastern Phoebe from 0.388 to 0.519. Those working open airspace did not move: Chimney Swift fell slightly, and the swallows stayed at or near zero throughout. That is the signature of insects increasing within the vegetation rather than a regional trend in the open air. Eastern Wood-Pewee is the exception, down from 0.408 to 0.231; pewees hunt from exposed perches in clear sub-canopy space, so a thickening understory may cost them the room they need.

The published mechanism

Non-native plants support far fewer insects than natives, because most herbivorous insects can only develop on plants they share an evolutionary history with. Narango, Tallamy and Marra (2018) tracked Carolina Chickadee reproduction and survival across a gradient of non-native planting and found that as non-native plants increased, both insect availability and chickadee population growth declined — and that populations could only be sustained where non-native plants made up less than 30% of plant biomass. A single nesting chickadee collects more than 400 caterpillars a day. Their conclusion was that yards dominated by non-native plants function as population sinks for insectivorous birds.

The distinction that follows matters for reading this study. Structure determines where a bird settles. Food determines whether it fledges young. A site with cover but no insect base is a trap rather than a habitat.

The weakest link in the chain

The causal chain this section proposes runs native plants → insects → insectivorous birds. Only the first and last steps are measured here. The middle step rests on the site manager's field observation of a marked increase in bees, butterflies and other insects since 2024, together with the published literature above. That observation comes from three years of close attention to the ground and is not lightly dismissed, but it is an impression rather than a count, and it now carries more of the argument than any other single piece of evidence.

Making it a measurement would be cheap. Pollinator counts along a fixed transect, or caterpillar beating-sheet samples on a set route in late May when biomass peaks, would take a few hours a season and would convert the central mechanism of this report from inference into data.

The same species can also be grouped by which band of the site they would occupy, which turns the record into something closer to a distance profile.

The garden band was already the richest part of the record in 2024, at three times the woodland figure and twenty times the open-ground figure, and it stayed richest. Some of that is genuine habitat quality and some is simply proximity, and a single microphone cannot separate the two.

The sequence then runs outward. The nearest and best-protected planting produced the earliest response, the woodland understory followed in 2025, and open-ground birds arrived only in 2026. That matches how quickly each zone could plausibly develop structure: fenced and closest first, unfenced and further second, remediated and furthest last.

Six of the nine birds that took up territory are garden and edge species, among them House Wren, Cedar Waxwing, Baltimore Oriole and White-breasted Nuthatch. Three are woodland interior: Wood Thrush, Scarlet Tanager and Great Crested Flycatcher. The headline arrivals are not spread evenly across the site, and the weight sits nearest the house.

The predators followed

Owls and hawks track prey. Their arrival is a reasonable proxy for whether the songbird and small mammal base underneath them has actually thickened.

Barred Owl is the most convincing of these. It hunts small mammals, amphibians and large invertebrates from perches in closed-canopy woodland with an open understory beneath. Restoring ground cover and leaf litter builds exactly the rodent and amphibian base it needs, and its increase is steady rather than sudden.

Together with Great Horned Owl, Broad-winged Hawk and Red-tailed Hawk, this is the top of the food web filling in behind the bottom of it. Osprey moves the other way, from 0.14 to nothing, which is a reservoir signal rather than a woodland one.

The meadow arrived late, and so did its birds

Zone D could not be sown until its drain field had been remediated. Seeding happened in 2025, which made 2026 the meadow's first established growing season.

Six birds jumped in 2026 after two flat years, and all six are early-successional shrubland and field-edge specialists.

Brown Thrasher 0.02 → 0.02 → 0.44  ·  Eastern Towhee 0.20 → 0.02 → 0.64  ·  Blue-winged Warbler 0 → 0 → 0.33
Eastern Bluebird 0.37 → 0.30 → 0.73  ·  Common Yellowthroat 0 → 0 → 0.06  ·  Indigo Bunting 0.02 → 0.04 → 0.10

Brown Thrasher and Eastern Towhee both feed by thrashing through litter at the base of dense shrubs, and both want a shrubby field edge rather than forest interior. Blue-winged Warbler is a specialist of exactly this habitat stage and one of the harder birds to attract deliberately. A grass and wildflower meadow with shrub clusters along its fence line is what recruits all three.

The timing points to the meadow. The location cannot be pinned to it. Zone C carries field grass openings of its own and sits far closer to the microphone, so some or all of these birds may be holding ground there rather than inside the fenced acre. What the data supports is that early-successional habitat came online somewhere on the site in 2026 and these six birds answered it.

Where the meadow effect stops

Field Sparrow sat at 0.06 throughout and never established. Prairie Warbler, Yellow Warbler and Chestnut-sided Warbler never registered at all. One acre is a small meadow and those species want larger, older, scrubbier ground. The recruitment is real but partial, and the fenced acre is probably close to its ceiling.

The planted food has not started working

Among several hundred plantings, two fruit crops appear on the plan in enough quantity to expect a measurable effect. Neither shows one yet, and that is the most useful negative result here.

Bayberry. Northern bayberry matters because its waxy fruit persists through winter. Two birds can digest that wax: Yellow-rumped Warbler, which is the reason it winters further north than any other warbler, and Tree Swallow. Across two winters, Yellow-rumped Warbler presence moved from 0.04 to 0.07 and Tree Swallow from 0.02 to zero. Winter 2025–26 was severe, which should have increased reliance on persistent fruit.

Blueberry. Across the July fruiting window, mean presence of blueberry-eating species was 0.559 in 2024 and 0.569 in 2025.

Both results are what the horticulture predicts. Highbush blueberry needs three to five years to bear meaningfully. Bayberry planted as small stock in 2024 is nowhere near a winter crop. What the birds have responded to so far is the insect food that native plantings generate almost immediately, together with the cover and nest sites that arrived alongside it. Fruit follows years behind both.

This connects directly back to Figure 2. The site has become a strong breeding-season habitat and remains a thin wintering one: of the twenty ground and understory feeders present in June, only three are still here in January. Fruit is the hinge. If the bayberry establishes, winter is where it will show.

Spruce removal changed nothing

Taking out the Zone B spruces should in principle have cost conifer specialists. Golden-crowned Kinglet went from 0.93 to 0.98 across winters, Red-breasted Nuthatch from 0.65 to 0.74, Brown Creeper from 0.85 to 0.86. Only a handful of trees came out, and a kinglet present on 98% of winter days indicates plenty of conifer still within earshot.

Two losses

One has a clear cause outside the project. The other does not yet have an explanation.

Carolina Wren. It does not migrate, and northern Westchester sits close to the northern limit of what it survives. Winter 2025–26 was New York's coldest and snowiest in eleven years: 43.4 inches of snow against a 29.8-inch normal, a weeks-long cold snap through January and February, a 19.7-inch blizzard on 22 February, and the coldest December since 2010. The decline runs progressively from the first winter onward. This is regional weather, and it would recover after one ordinary winter.

Ovenbird. Ovenbird was the strongest arrival of 2025, going from absent to present on 44% of breeding-season days, then fell to 9.6% in 2026. It is a ground-walking warbler that needs open, leaf-littered floor beneath a closed canopy, and it holds small territories in fixed positions.

The cause is not recoverable from a single year. A pair shifting territory a hundred metres would produce this pattern; so would a real change in ground conditions; so would the classifier change, which falls in the same window. It is flagged here because a large one-year reversal in a species of regional conservation concern is worth following, not because the reason is known.

The breeding community grew by half. Winter did not move, and its composition is close to frozen: the same nine bark foragers, the same eleven on seed and mast. Only predators, from five species to seven, and fruit-eaters, from two to three, shifted at all. What winters here is the community your mature oaks were already supporting — trunks, branches, acorns and seed heads.

The reason is that the planting delivered two kinds of food on very different schedules. Native plants begin supporting insects almost at once: herbaceous natives and young shrubs carry caterpillars, bees and flies within a season or two. That is summer food, and the breeding community answered it. Fruit runs on a much slower clock, because woody plants need three to five years to bear meaningfully. That is winter food, and it has not arrived.

The twenty ground and shrub feeders that define the breeding season here are almost all insectivores, and a northeastern winter removes their food supply entirely. Wood Thrush leaves for Central America, Ovenbird for the Caribbean. They would leave a perfect hillside too, and their departure is not a failure of the planting.

Only three of the twenty stay, and how they manage it is the whole argument. American Robin and Hermit Thrush both switch to fruit in winter — Hermit Thrush is the only thrush of its genus that regularly winters this far north, and it does so on fruit. Carolina Wren does not migrate at all and gleans invertebrates from bark and tangles year-round, which is why it is the one being killed by hard winters rather than leaving before them.

Fruit is the only door from a shrub layer into winter

Nothing else about shrub structure feeds a bird in January. Two independent measurements agree that door is barely open: the seasonal census, where winter sits flat and its guild structure has not reshuffled, and the bayberry indicator, where Yellow-rumped Warbler holds at 0.07 and Tree Swallow at zero.

There is a faint signal the other way, and it deserves stating rather than burying. Fruit-eaters are the only group besides predators to move at all: American Robin 0.26 to 0.43 across the two winters, Cedar Waxwing 0.03 to 0.15, Eastern Bluebird 0.12 to 0.18. That could be the first fruit arriving. It could equally be a severe winter concentrating birds onto whatever fruit already existed. Two winters cannot separate the two.

What this implies for planting

Of the two fruit crops named on the plan, only one is a winter crop. Blueberry is stripped by August; it is a summer resource that will show up in July frugivore numbers and contribute nothing in January. Bayberry persists because its wax coating resists rot. The entire winter pathway currently rests on the bayberry establishing.

The mechanism is worth understanding before adding anything, because it is counter-intuitive. Winter-persistent fruits persist precisely because birds do not much want them. They are low in sugar and fat, and often astringent. Birds strip the sweet, high-fat fruit first — spicebush, dogwood, blueberry — and leave the sour ones standing until February, when nothing else is left. That unpalatability is the feature, not a defect.

Species that behave this way and are documented native to New York State:

  • Winterberry (Ilex verticillata) — the standard for winter persistence, taken by robins and mockingbirds in late winter. Dioecious: a female-only planting sets no fruit.
  • Staghorn and smooth sumac (Rhus typhina, R. glabra) — passed over in autumn, eaten in deep winter by woodpeckers, chickadees, nuthatches, sparrows and waxwings. That is precisely the guild already wintering on this site.
  • Red and black chokeberry (Aronia arbutifolia, A. melanocarpa) — astringent, so they hold well into winter.
  • Eastern red cedar (Juniperus virginiana) — also dioecious. Paired with bayberry it is the combination that supports wintering Tree Swallow and Cedar Waxwing, two of the species this study is watching.
  • Blackhaw, nannyberry and American cranberrybush viburnum (Viburnum prunifolium, V. lentago, V. opulus var. americanum) — blackhaw fruit is taken by Hermit Thrush, Eastern Bluebird, Northern Cardinal, Cedar Waxwing and White-throated Sparrow, five birds already on the winter list.
  • Native roses (Rosa carolina, R. virginiana, R. palustris) — hips persist and are taken by blackbirds, grackles and sparrows.

Deliberately excluded

American holly (Ilex opaca) appears on most regional winter-berry lists, but its native range in New York is the southern part of the state and it is not native this far north. Ornamental crabapples are almost all non-native hybrids. Rosa and Viburnum both contain widely sold non-natives and outright invasives — multiflora and rugosa rose, Siebold's and linden viburnum — so both genera should be bought by botanical name rather than by common name. Given that this project exists to remove invasive shrubs, that distinction is not academic. Viburnum leaf beetle is also well established in New York and hits some native viburnums hard, which is worth checking against local nursery advice before planting a quantity of them.

One caveat on the whole argument. Fifty species may be close to the ceiling for a northern Westchester woodland in January, in which case there is little headroom regardless of what is planted. What suggests otherwise is that the winter structure is frozen rather than reshuffling: the same guilds at the same sizes, two years running, while the breeding season moved sharply. That looks more like a door that has not opened than a room that is full.

What to listen for next

Winter total breaking 52. Winter has sat at 50 to 52 species for two years while the breeding season rose by half. If fruit comes into bearing around 2027 to 2029, winter should break above that band for the first time, and the fruit-eating group should exceed three species. If winter is still near 50 in 2029, the shrub layer is not converting into winter food and the planting palette is the reason.

Yellow-rumped Warbler in winter. If the bayberry fruits, winter presence should climb well above 0.07. This is the single most diagnostic measure of whether the planting has begun producing food rather than only cover. Watch winters from 2027–28.

Blueberry-eaters separating in late July. First meaningful highbush crops fall around 2027–29. Expect Gray Catbird, Brown Thrasher, Veery and Cedar Waxwing to pull away from non-fruit-eating species specifically inside the fruiting window.

Ovenbird. Whether the 2026 drop holds or reverses is the cheapest question on this list to answer, and it settles whether 2025 was the anomaly or 2026 was.

Carolina Wren recovering. One mild winter should bring it back toward its 2024 level within two breeding seasons. If it does not, the weather reading here was wrong and something else is happening.

Appendix

Method and limits

Measure

A single Haikubox, fixed to the house wall and never moved, recording continuously from 2 June 2024 to 25 July 2026: 775 days with data across a 784-day span, 1.55 million detections, 254 proposed species names. The unit throughout is a species-day — whether a bird was present on a given day, as a share of days with data in a matched calendar window. A day counts as presence only if the species was detected at least five times that day. Validated against the score-bearing exports, a five-detection day carries a 67% probability of containing a high-confidence identification against 8% for a single-detection day. Nothing here infers how many individuals were present; a flock and one persistent singer are not distinguishable on a microphone.

Credibility screening

Of 254 proposed names, 111 were judged plausible for an interior Westchester upland adjoining a large reservoir and carried into the analysis. Rejected high-volume identifications include Barn Owl (476 days), White-crowned Sparrow (408), Red-headed Woodpecker (297), Monk Parakeet (92), Grasshopper Sparrow (62) and Dunlin (61). The adjoining reservoir makes kingfisher, osprey, heron, waterfowl, gull and swallow records legitimate; it does not make coastal species legitimate.

Limits

  • Recording began after clearance and planting were complete. There is no pre-treatment baseline, so this measures how a young restoration matured, not the effect of removing invasives.
  • No control site and no matched untreated comparison. Regional population trends and weather years are unmeasured. Breeding Bird Survey or eBird data for northern Westchester County would supply a pseudo-control and is the most valuable single addition available.
  • The platform's classifier changed between the September 2025 and June 2026 exports. The 2024-to-2025 comparison is within one version; the 2025-to-2026 comparison is not, and year-three-only changes cannot be fully separated from a model effect. Where that applies it is noted in the text.
  • No detection has been verified by a human listener; the device retains no audio for review.
  • Detection distances are estimated from published passive-acoustic range studies in comparable forest, not measured on site.
  • Feeding-group and credibility assignments are editorial, following published species accounts and standard trait groupings.
